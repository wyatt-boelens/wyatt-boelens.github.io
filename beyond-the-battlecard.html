CASE STUDY — GTM RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Beyond the Battlecard

A framework for building outreach copy that sounds like it was written by someone who's actually sat in the prospect's seat — not just read a one-page battlecard about it.

Genericized for portfolio purposes. The six-part framework and the quality-bar principles below are real and mine. The worked example is freshly written for this page — not a copy of any real, proven line, since publishing those verbatim would mean giving away an earned asset, not just removing a company name.

The problem

A standard ICP battlecard gives you goals and pains at a glance — enough to write something that's technically relevant. It's rarely enough to write something that reads as though the writer actually understands the job. Most outreach that gets ignored isn't wrong, exactly, it's just generic: it could have been sent to five other titles without changing a word.

The gap between "relevant" and "sounds like an insider" isn't effort, it's specificity — the exact number someone reports upward, the word choice that signals fluency versus the word choice that flags a vendor, the objection they've actually heard a dozen times and the honest (not deflected) answer to it.

The framework
01
Avatar

A short sketch of the actual day-to-day and what's weighing on this person right now — not a job description, a lived-in situation.

02
The KPI they report upward

The specific number that defines "value" to them, precisely worded — precision here matters more than it sounds like it should.

03
Jargon

The words that signal "this person gets my job" versus the words that flag an outside vendor. Used directly, not paraphrased into safer, more generic language.

04
How they define value

What actually lands for this specific seat — not a generic benefit statement that could apply to any role in the function.

05
Objections, paired with honest answers

Real pushback this role actually raises, paired with a defensible response — not a deflection. If the honest answer requires data that isn't available, the rule is to flag that gap rather than invent a number to fill it.

06
Anchor lines

Proven lines, tested in real conversations — the payoff of the first five sections, not a separate creative exercise bolted on top of them.

07
Pressure-test before delivery

A well-crafted line that doesn't anticipate the objection it's about to trigger is still flat-footed. Before anything gets delivered, it's read back as the persona would read it — genuinely skeptical — checked against the catalogued objections, and revised if it doesn't already preempt or productively invite the reaction it's walking into. One pass minimum, two at most; if the first pass finds nothing to tighten, that's the answer, not a reason to force a second edit.

The quality bar

One test decides whether a line is actually using the persona work or just referencing it: would this sentence only make sense to someone in this exact role, or could it apply to any job in the function? If the answer is the latter, the research didn't actually make it into the copy yet.

Could apply to any marketing role
"We help you lower acquisition costs and grow your subscriber base."
Only makes sense to this exact seat
"Of the acquisition channels you've added this year, which ones are still pulling their weight instead of quietly fading?"
Worked example — Growth Marketer

One persona, fully built out, for a fictional subscription-marketplace seller ("Loopline"):

Avatar

Owns the paid acquisition budget and watches CAC weekly, sometimes daily mid-push. Has added two or three "new" channels in the past couple of years — each looked promising for a quarter, then CAC drifted back up as more competitors piled into the same auction. Dreads explaining to leadership why diversification didn't actually move the number.

KPI they report upward

Blended CAC — not "average CAC." The distinction matters: blended signals someone thinking about the whole acquisition budget hitting the P&L, not one channel in isolation. Using the wrong term here is a quiet, immediate tell that the message came from outside the function.

Jargon (used directly, not softened)

Blended CAC, CAC payback period, channel mix, audience saturation, auction dynamics, channel diversification, plateauing. Avoid "marketing spend" — too broad, reads as though it includes brand/content budget rather than the specific paid-media line actually inflating.

How they define value

A channel that's structurally different from what they're already running — not another platform inside the same auction dynamic. Something with a quantifiable CAC impact they can put on a slide and defend without hand-waving.

Objection + honest answer

"Won't this just get crowded and expensive over time, like every other channel?"
Fair pushback, and the honest answer has to actually engage with it: a channel with no bidding and no auction isn't structurally the same mechanism as paid media, which is the real reason it wouldn't erode the same way — not a marketing claim asserted without a mechanism behind it.

First draft

"Loopline gives you a growth channel that doesn't behave like a normal paid channel."

Pressure-test — read as this skeptical Growth Marketer

"Sure, but isn't that what everyone says about every new channel right before it gets crowded and expensive too?" The catalogued objection, and the draft doesn't actually preempt it — it asserts the outcome without naming the mechanism behind it.

Revised anchor line

"There's no bidding and no auction here — that's the actual mechanism that keeps this from eroding the way paid channels always do, not just a claim about the outcome."

Objection check

Pressure-tested against: "won't this get crowded and expensive over time too." The original named the right outcome but not the reason for it — revised to lead with the structural mechanism (no auction), which is what actually survives a skeptical read. One pass was enough; a second pass found nothing further to tighten.

Why this matters

Generic outreach fails quietly — it doesn't bounce, it just doesn't land, and it's hard to tell the difference from the sending side. Building this level of specificity per role is slower than writing a single company-wide template, but it's the difference between copy that reads as informed and copy that reads as a mail merge with a name swapped in.

Where this stops

This framework supplies content — the words, the angle, the objection handling. It doesn't own tone rules, sequence architecture, or CTA structure, which stay governed separately so persona-specific content never overrides house style. And when an honest answer to an objection would require real data that isn't on hand, the rule is to flag the gap explicitly rather than invent a number that sounds plausible — a persuasive-sounding fabrication is worse than an honest "I'd need to check."
