The Buying Committee Router

A tool that reads a list of prospects and figures out, one by one, who's likely to actually make the decision versus who's just in the loop — then writes a first message suited to that role. Built to keep outreach personal at a volume that would normally take additional headcount to manage by hand.

Genericized reconstruction for portfolio purposes. The role-classification, sequencing, and proof-point logic is real and mine; the seller, reference customers, and prospects below are fictional.

The problem

Tools like ZoomInfo, Apollo, and Sales Navigator are good at finding people who fit a target profile. They don't tell you which of those people actually makes the decision, which ones just influence it, and which ones have no real say — that part still happens by hand, one profile at a time.

That manual step is where scale actually breaks:

  • Reviewing a hundred contacts one by one to figure out who's worth prioritizing takes real time, even for someone good at it.
  • Skipping that step and messaging everyone the same way gets weaker replies and wastes outreach on people who can't say yes anyway.

This exists to remove that manual step. It takes an already-sourced list and applies the same rules every time to identify each person's likely role, flags the ones that need a second look, and writes a first message suited to that role — without needing additional headcount just to keep up with the review.

The architecture
01
Title sets a starting guess, not a confirmed fact
  • True C-suite titles (CEO, CFO, Founder, and similar) are marked as Economic Buyer.
  • VP, SVP, and EVP titles are marked as Executive Sponsor.
  • Directors and Heads in the relevant function are marked as Champion.
  • Individual contributors in that function are marked as End User.
  • IT, Security, Ops, and Procurement titles are marked as Technical Evaluator, regardless of level — who evaluates a tool doesn't scale with seniority the way who approves the budget does.

Every role assignment comes with the specific question a rep should ask to actually confirm it. A title is a strong guess, not proof — MEDDIC discipline exists specifically to stop people from treating a guess as settled fact.

02
Use the closest matching reference customer, not just the same industry label

First, look for a reference customer in the exact same industry. If there isn't a good one, use the closest match instead — a company of a similar size, or one that sells in a similar way — rather than picking any company in the same industry just because the label matches.

03
Weak fits get flagged, and they actually get treated differently

Weak fits still show up on the list, clearly flagged — but they get a short, simple line instead of a fully written message. A flag that doesn't change what happens next is just decoration. This one does change it: less effort goes toward contacts unlikely to matter, so more time goes toward the ones who do.

04
People at the same company get sequenced together, not scored one by one

When two or more people from the same company are on the list, they're not treated as separate, unrelated contacts. The system recommends who to reach out to first — because some roles are better positioned to share useful information early, and that information makes the message to the next person sharper.

05
Output is a table, ready to use immediately

The output is a table, not a paragraph — built to drop straight into a CRM or outreach tool with no extra formatting step. Role, proof point, pain angle, and opening message each get their own column.

06
Pull live signals when the list is worth the extra time
  • Not every prospect needs research, but the system can look one up — recent LinkedIn posts, company news, press releases.
  • When it finds something real and current, that shapes the opening line instead of using a generic one.
  • When it doesn't find anything useful, it says so honestly and falls back to the role-based approach rather than making something up.
Why this matters

A skilled rep makes this judgment call naturally, prospect by prospect. This turns that same judgment into a rule set that works the same way at 5 prospects or 500 — the quality doesn't drop as the list gets longer, which is usually exactly where manual personalization falls apart.

Where this stops

This identifies who someone likely is on the deal — it doesn't try to guess what they're currently thinking, and it doesn't replace an actual conversation. MEDDIC's Decision Criteria and Decision Process, for example, can only be confirmed by talking to the prospect directly; no title-based or research-based system should claim to know those in advance, and this one doesn't try.

A future version could get sharper with use over time — tracking which proof points and reframes actually get replies, instead of staying static.
